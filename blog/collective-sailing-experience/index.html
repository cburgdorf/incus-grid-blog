Sailing routes: collective sailing experience, made explorable

Discover the routes other sailors take, compare seasonal patterns in both directions, and explore thousands of real recorded journeys with IncusGrid.

Where do other sailors cross from Sicily to Greece? Which connections do they use between the islands of the Aegean? And how does the traffic change through the year?

These are the questions we keep asking when we plan our own sailing. A chart shows the geography. A forecast helps us choose a weather window. But there is another useful piece of the picture: the journeys other sailors have actually made.

Sailing Routes brings that collective experience to IncusGrid. Built from thousands of real recorded boat movements, the layer lets you explore which connections sailors use, how often those connections appear in the data, and when activity tends to peak.

Which route, as well as when

Ask a sailor why they chose a particular passage and the answer rarely comes down to distance alone. Wind direction matters. So does the shape of the coast, the next useful stop, or the prospect of a sheltered arrival after a long crossing.

Those choices can leave patterns in the journeys people record. Popular connections offer a starting point for exploring the accumulated experience behind them.

If you have spent time with a book of ocean passages or world cruising routes, the idea will feel familiar. We wanted to make that curiosity interactive: move around the map, look at the connections, and compare the seasons for yourself.

The data cannot tell us why each skipper made a decision. A busy route is an invitation to investigate, rather than proof that it suits every boat or set of conditions.

Sicily to Greece in spring. The other direction in autumn.

One of our favourite examples is the connection between Sicily and the Ionian Islands.

Select the eastbound route and the seasonal chart shows activity rising in spring and early summer. Switch direction and the westbound pattern peaks in autumn. The contrast is much more useful than a single annual total: the same stretch of water tells a different story depending on which way you are going.

These are patterns across recorded journeys; they do not establish that the same boats made a round trip. Months are grouped by the first recorded arrival at the destination, which matters especially for longer passages that span two months.

Sicily and Greece are just one example. You can explore ocean passages, regional crossings and shorter coastal connections in the same map.

Popular passages and less-travelled connections

The Route popularity slider lets you choose how much detail to explore.

Move it towards Only more popular to focus on connections supported by more boats. Move it towards Include less popular to reveal more of the quieter connections. Zoom in to explore shorter routes around a cruising area.

In the Aegean, this changes the question from “How do people get across this sea?” to “Which connections do people use around these islands?” It makes the feature useful for the next part of a coastal cruise as well as a longer passage.

The filter controls what is visible; it does not rank routes by safety or difficulty. Less-recorded routes may simply be less represented in the source data.

Follow your curiosity beyond familiar waters

Some of the most exciting discoveries start with a place you had never thought to look. Follow recorded connections along Alaska’s coast near the Bering Strait, explore the passage from Pitcairn to the Gambier Islands in the South Pacific, or trace the link between the Cocos (Keeling) Islands and Rodrigues across the Indian Ocean. These are all connections you can find in IncusGrid, built from real recorded boat movements.

Zooming out puts those tiny islands in perspective. Zooming back in reveals the journeys connecting them, and suddenly a remote speck on the map becomes a place to explore. Even if your next passage is closer to home, there is something exciting about discovering where other sailors have been.

Try a route you know

  1. Open IncusGrid and select Sailing Routes.
  2. Start with Year to explore the connections in an area you know.
  3. Adjust Route popularity until you have a useful amount of detail.
  4. Select a connection to inspect its seasonal pattern. Where a counterpart is available, use Switch direction to compare it.
  5. Choose a month on the timeline, or use Share to send someone a link to the connection you are exploring.

The lines are schematic connections reconstructed from recorded position reports, not exact navigational tracks. Use them to discover patterns, then bring charts, forecasts and your own passage planning to the decision.
