Two seas, two clocks: How the time of day changes sailing in Greece

Same country, same month, completely different rhythm. With IncusGrid's new time-of-day wind layers we compare the Ionian and the Aegean in July — and find out where the wind sleeps at night and where it never does.

Ask ten sailors about Greece in July and you will hear two very different stories. One group talks about lazy mornings, a reliable afternoon breeze and glassy anchorages at night. The other group talks about the Meltemi: 20 knots from the north, the rigging howling all night and a crew checking the anchor at 3 am.

Both groups are right. They just sailed on different seas. And the difference isn't only how much wind there is. It's when it blows.

IncusGrid's wind layers can now be split by time of day: Morning (06–10), Midday (10–14), Afternoon (14–18), Evening (18–22) and Night (22–06), always in local clock time. We also added Mean speed, Steadiness and Quiet nights. The data comes from a weather model at roughly 9 km resolution, hourly from 2017 to 2025.

We clicked through July in Greece. Here's what we found.

The afternoon picture

Start with the classic view: mean wind speed in July, afternoon hours.

Nothing surprising so far. The Aegean is the windy one, with a band of 14+ knots running from the Dardanelles down through the Cyclades to Crete. The Ionian side, west of mainland Greece, looks gentle by comparison.

But an afternoon map hides half the story. Let's change the clock.

The Ionian: a sea that wakes up at lunchtime

Zoom in on Lefkada, Meganisi and Kefalonia and switch between Morning and Afternoon.

In the channel south of Meganisi, the average July morning brings 4 knots. By the afternoon it's 10.7 knots, most often from the west. This is the well-known Maistros, a thermal breeze that builds as the land heats up and fades in the evening. Averaged over the whole Ionian between Lefkada and Zakynthos, wind speed climbs from about 6 knots in the morning to nearly 10 knots in the late afternoon and evening.

This isn't just a model result. The airport weather station at Preveza/Aktion, just north of Lefkada, measured the same rhythm in July 2017–2025: about 4 knots in the morning and 12 knots in the afternoon.

What this means for planning: Motor or ghost along in the morning, sail in the afternoon, and have the anchor down before dinner. That's not folklore. It's what nine years of hourly data say.

The Aegean: a sea without a clock

Now do the same in the Cyclades. Morning, midday, afternoon, evening, night: in the open water between Syros, Mykonos and Paros the map barely changes. Averaged over the Cyclades in July, mean wind speed is 13.6 knots in the morning, 14.0 in the afternoon and 13.2 at night. That's a difference of less than one knot around the clock.

The Meltemi isn't a sea breeze. It's driven by the large-scale pressure difference between the Balkans and the heat low over Turkey and the Middle East, and that doesn't switch off when the sun goes down.

At midnight east of Syros the average is still 13.5 knots, the same as at 3 pm.

One caveat matters for anyone at anchor: this flat profile holds on open water. Close to the islands, the land does put a small daily rhythm on top. The weather station at Mykonos airport measured about 17 knots on July afternoons and 11 knots at night. So the wind eases a little in a bay at night, but it rarely goes to sleep.

Quiet nights: where the wind sleeps

For anyone at anchor, the more useful question is: How likely is a calm night? That's what the new Quiet nights layer shows: the share of nights in which the wind stays below 10 knots from 22:00 to 06:00 the whole time.

This is the map that best captures the two stories. A pale corridor runs straight down the Aegean, where quiet nights are rare. Almost everything else is purple: the Ionian, the Gulf of Corinth, the Saronic Gulf, the Gulf of Volos and many sheltered pockets in the lee of larger islands.

Tap two points and the contrast becomes concrete:

In July, about 17% of nights east of Syros are quiet. In the Meganisi channel the model shows 88%. Treat that as "most nights" rather than an exact figure: the nearest weather station at Preveza measured about three quiet nights in four, while the stations on Mykonos, Syros and Santorini measured only one in four. Over the regions as a whole, the Cyclades average 19%, the Ionian 62% and the Saronic Gulf, right on Athens' doorstep, 74%.

The monthly bars tell you something else too: in the Cyclades, quiet nights peak in May (about 38% on average across the islands, twice the July value). If you want the Cyclades with fewer windy nights at anchor, go early.

Steadiness: the Meltemi as a conveyor belt

The Steadiness layer shows how consistently the wind keeps one direction, with arrows pointing where it blows to.

In July the Cyclades score 87 out of 100. The arrows form a near-perfect conveyor belt toward the south-southwest. That's great news for a passage from Mykonos to Santorini and hard work for the reverse trip. In May the same area scores only 49: winds are lighter and far more variable.

The Ionian sits at 70: a fairly steady direction, but much less strength.

A surprise in the North Aegean

We expected the North Aegean to behave like the Cyclades. It doesn't. On the open water between Lemnos and the Dardanelles, July mornings are the windiest part of the day and the afternoon is the calmest.

At one point east of Lemnos the average drops from 14.2 knots in the morning to 8.5 knots in the afternoon, then picks up again overnight. That's the opposite of the Ionian rhythm.

This one deserves a note of caution. A second, independent model shows the same pattern at the same spot (13.4 knots in the morning, 8.1 in the afternoon). But there is no weather station out there to confirm it, and the station at Lemnos airport, on land, shows the usual afternoon peak. So this looks like an open-water effect that fades near the coast. We won't pretend to have the full meteorological explanation. For planning, the takeaway is simple: on a North Aegean crossing, the "wait for the morning calm" rule can backfire.

The Dodecanese, by contrast, follows the Ionian pattern on top of the Meltemi: around Kos and Kalymnos wind builds from about 11 knots in the morning to 14 knots in the evening.

How we checked

Model maps are only useful if they match reality, so we compared them with what was actually measured. For July 2017–2025 we pulled the hourly reports from 14 airport weather stations around the Aegean and the Ionian and compared them with the IncusGrid values at the same locations. For the open-water points, we also compared against a second, coarser weather model.

The daily rhythm at Preveza, the round-the-clock wind at Mykonos, Syros and Santorini, and the contrast in quiet nights all show up in the measurements.

What we take away

  • Ionian: a daily rhythm. Calm mornings, a west-to-northwest afternoon breeze and mostly quiet nights. Ideal for relaxed cruising and families.
  • Cyclades: no real rhythm on open water in July. The Meltemi blows morning to night from the north. In anchorages it eases a little at night, but quiet nights stay rare. Plan your anchorages for north wind, every night.
  • North Aegean: on open water, a reversed rhythm. Mornings can be the roughest part of the day.
  • Shoulder season: in May the Cyclades offer twice as many quiet nights as in July.

All of these are long-term averages, not forecasts. A single July day in the Ionian can still blow 25 knots, and the Cyclades do get calm spells. But knowing the typical rhythm of a sea helps you choose where to go, when to leave and where to spend the night.
