Preveza or Mesolóngi? Thunderstorm risk where boats spend the winter

Our new Thunderstorm Risk layer combines thunderstorm days, lightning density and terrain into one value per month, down to single bays. A look at the Mediterranean's wintering harbours, and how all of IncusGrid's thunderstorm layers fit together.

In October 2024 our family sheltered from a storm in the bay of Igoumenitsa, in the Ionian Sea, when lightning struck the water right next to our boat. Since then one question has stayed with us: would we have been safer somewhere else? On the other side of the bay, in the next bay, in another harbour?

Until now IncusGrid could answer half of it. Our thunderstorm layers showed how often storms occur in a sea area of about 50 × 50 km, and Terrain Exposure showed which side of a bay is better shielded. What was missing was the step in between: is this harbour more exposed than the one a few miles away?

That is what our new layer, Thunderstorm Risk, is for.

Preveza or Mesolóngi?

Every autumn crews decide where to leave the boat for the winter. In our look at Mediterranean wintering spots, Preveza came out as the clear number one, and Mesolóngi, about 47 nautical miles to the southeast, is in the top ten too.

Tap Preveza in November, then Mesolóngi, and IncusGrid compares the two: Mesolóngi has 27 % less thunderstorm risk. Preveza's risk value is 50, Mesolóngi's 36.

November is no coincidence. In the Ionian, autumn is thunderstorm season: the sea is still warm, and the first winter lows bring cold air over it. At Preveza, November has the highest risk of the whole year, about twice as high as October or December.

That doesn't make Preveza a bad choice. It means a boat left there in November should be well prepared, and that a crew still living aboard may want to know how much the place next door differs.

What Thunderstorm Risk combines

Thunderstorm Risk brings three things together in one value:

  • How often thunderstorms occur at a place: the share of days with lightning, from the WWLLN lightning network, 2013–2025.
  • How violent they are there: how much lightning a typical storm day brings.
  • How the terrain shapes them. Mountains, headlands and open water make thunderstorms form more often in some spots than in others, and depending on the season, more over land or more over the sea. A model trained on more than a decade of lightning observations and detailed elevation data takes this into account for every month.

The result is a map along the coasts, about 80 km out to sea and 30 km inland, with differences down to single bays. It is made for comparing: which harbour is better this month, which month is calmer at this place? Tap one place, then a second one, and you see how much more or less risk the second has. Differences below 10 % count as about the same.

Four patterns stand out:

  • The Ionian peaks in November and stays at a fairly high level through the winter.
  • Sicily and Malta also peak in October and November, then the risk drops to about a third in December.
  • Leros turns the calendar around: low in early autumn, highest in January. The Aegean's thunderstorm season is the winter.
  • Southeast Spain and Tunisia are calm. Almerimar and Cartagena have about a tenth of Preveza's November risk, and in Tunisia the risk is highest in September and falls through the winter.

How often, how violent, how likely

Behind Thunderstorm Risk are three layers that you can open on their own, each on the same scale in every month. They answer different questions, and in September the Mediterranean shows why all three matter.

Thunderstorm Days show how often thunderstorms occur: the share of days with lightning in an area of about 50 × 50 km. In September they are most frequent along the Adriatic and Ionian coasts.

Lightning Density shows how violent they are: the average number of lightning strokes per 100 km² and month. In September the lightning is densest over the sea between Italy and Greece. An area with few thunderstorm days but a high density has rare but strong storms.

Thunderstorm Potential shows where thunderstorms can build: the share of time with conditions that favour them, from ERA5 weather data 1994–2023. In September these conditions reach far into the south, including places where storms often could have formed but didn't.

From the season to the anchor spot

Put together, the layers take you from the big picture to the place where you drop anchor:

  1. The season and the sea area: Thunderstorm Days, Lightning Density and Thunderstorm Potential, month by month.
  2. The harbour or the bay: Thunderstorm Risk, down to single bays.
  3. The side of the bay: Terrain Exposure, which shows how well the surrounding hills shield each spot on the water.

Try it yourself

  1. Open the Lightning group and choose Thunderstorm Risk.
  2. Pick a month on the timeline.
  3. Tap a harbour or bay, then a second one, and compare.

Compare thunderstorm risk on IncusGrid →

A few honest caveats: Thunderstorm Risk is an estimate from many years of data, not a forecast for a particular day, and its values are made for comparing places and months, not as absolute probabilities. Further inland the layer shows no values. And lightning is chaotic: a better place lowers the odds, it doesn't rule anything out. Always check the current forecast before you go to sea.
