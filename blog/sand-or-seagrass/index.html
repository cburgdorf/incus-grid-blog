Sand or seagrass? Finding a good anchoring spot in the Mediterranean

Anchoring on Posidonia is banned in more and more places, and the difference between sand and seagrass is hard to see from the cockpit. Our new Seabed layer shows the type of bottom along the coast, from the Balearics to the Caribbean.

A turquoise bay, the anchor ready, and the question every Mediterranean skipper knows: is that sand down there, or seagrass? From the cockpit the dark patches all look alike, and in the afternoon light you often cannot tell at all.

It matters more every year, and it is why we built our newest layer: Seabed.

Why Posidonia matters

Posidonia oceanica is a seagrass that only grows in the Mediterranean. Its meadows keep the water clear, protect the beaches from erosion and are nurseries for fish. They grow extremely slowly, so an anchor and chain dragging through a meadow do damage that takes decades to heal.

That is why the rules are getting stricter:

  • Balearic Islands: since a 2018 decree, anchoring on Posidonia is banned for all boats, whatever their size. In summer, environmental patrol boats check the anchorages around Mallorca, Ibiza, Formentera and Menorca and ask crews to move.
  • France: along the Mediterranean coast, yachts over 24 m (in some areas 20 m) may no longer anchor in seagrass meadows, and the fines are serious.
  • Elsewhere in Spain, Italy, Croatia and Greece, protected areas and local rules apply.

The rules change and differ from place to place, so check the local regulations before you go. What all of them have in common: drop the anchor on sand.

The Seabed layer

The new Seabed layer in IncusGrid colours the coastal waters by the type of bottom: sand, mud, gravel, rock, seagrass, dead Posidonia matte, reef or coral, and rubble. In Europe it covers the coasts down to anchoring depths; in the tropics it covers shallow coastal waters, such as the Caribbean, the Red Sea and the South Pacific.

Two kinds of colour tell you how reliable the map is:

  • Strong colours are mapped from surveys or satellite images.
  • Pale colours are only rough estimates from a model, about 100 m across.

Tap a spot and IncusGrid tells you the type of bottom there.

The Balearics: green almost everywhere close to shore

Zoom out over the Balearics and one thing stands out: around Mallorca, Ibiza and Formentera the shallow water close to the coast is green for long stretches. The meadows reach right up to the beaches in many bays, which is exactly where you would like to anchor.

The channel between Ibiza and Formentera, around Espalmador, is one large meadow. It is one of the best known Posidonia areas in the Mediterranean, and one of the busiest anchorages in summer.

Spain's Costa Blanca: Dénia and Xàbia

On the mainland the picture is similar. North of Dénia and around the headlands towards Xàbia, the meadows lie close to the shore; further out and in parts of the bays, the bottom is sand or mud.

And in the tropics

The same layer works far beyond the Mediterranean. In the Bahamas, for example, the shallow banks are a patchwork of sand and seagrass, with reefs along the edges, which is the kind of information you want before anchoring off an Exuma cay.

How to use it

  1. Open the Seabed layer in IncusGrid. It is free; after a few taps the app asks for a free account.
  2. Zoom into the bay you are planning to anchor in.
  3. Look for the sand: strong yellow is mapped sand, pale yellow an estimate.
  4. Tap a spot to see the type of bottom there.

Open the Seabed layer on IncusGrid →

The Seabed layer is not a chart, and seagrass maps are never complete. Use it to plan where to look, then do what experienced crews do anyway: look at the bottom before you drop the hook, and respect protected seagrass and coral.
