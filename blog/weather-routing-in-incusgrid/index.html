From two clicks to a passage plan: weather routing in IncusGrid

IncusGrid now plans passages on the 10-day forecast. Set a start and a destination, and the planner calculates a fast route for your boat: arrival time, strongest wind, hours under sail and under motor, and departure times to compare.

Two days ago we put the 10-day forecast on the map. The next question on board is usually a practical one: if we leave tomorrow, when do we arrive, and what will the wind be like on the way?

The new passage planner answers that. You set a start and a destination, and IncusGrid calculates a fast route for your boat using the forecast, working out the passage hour by hour.

We'll show it on a passage in the Dodecanese: Leros to Rhodes, past Kos, around Cape Krio and south of Symi. It is about 87 nautical miles, or around 16 hours for a 40-foot cruiser. In November there are only about eleven hours of daylight, so part of the passage will be at night whenever you leave. Our example starts on Wednesday, 4 November 2026; all times here are local to Greece (UTC+2).

Planning the passage

Open Plan a passage in the layer bar, next to Wind. Click the map for the start, then for the destination. Set both just outside the harbour: the planner routes at sea and leaves the last miles into port to you. It names the passage after the nearest places, and you can rename it.

Under Boat, choose the type and size that comes closest to the boat you sail, for example Monohull 38–42 ft. The planner uses its typical speeds to time the passage, and for a first plan that is all you need.

Then choose a departure anywhere in the forecast and press Calculate route. After a few seconds the summary appears at the top of the planner:

  • Arrival, marked with ☾ when it falls after dark or at dusk
  • Strongest wind, gusts and waves on the way
  • Sailing: the hours upwind, reaching and downwind
  • Motoring: the hours under engine, and roughly how much fuel that uses

On the map the route is coloured by wind speed, motoring is dashed white and night hours are shaded. Every course change is a waypoint with the time you pass it. Under each leg you can open its hours with speed, wind, and true and apparent wind angle. Positions are in degrees and minutes, as on the plotter.

With the slider under Along the route you can follow the boat along the passage on the map. Next to it you see its speed, the wind angles, wind, gusts and waves. The wind map moves to the same hour, so you also see the weather around the route.

Adjusting the boat

The boat settings affect both the route and its timing. If you know your boat well, you can adjust it under Polar and settings: the polar table itself, how efficiently you sail with a full cruising load, how close to the wind you go, a wind speed to stay out of, when you start the engine, and its speed and fuel use. With an account your boat is saved and available on every device you sign in on.

When to leave

Leaving Leros on Wednesday morning gives a good downwind sail, but it arrives in Rhodes at a quarter past one at night, after 17 hours at sea.

Compare departures checks up to twelve departure times, six hours apart, starting at your selected departure and stopping at the end of the forecast. It lists arrival, duration, strongest wind and time under motor. Among completed passages, the star marks the lowest combined time under motor and upwind, rounded to whole hours. If that is tied, the shorter passage wins. Click a row to see that route on the map.

In this table the star is on Thursday at 14:00: 13 hours without the engine. It arrives at ten to three in the morning, though. Wednesday evening also has no motoring or upwind sailing, but Thursday wins because the passage is shorter. The star does not account for daylight at arrival, so it is worth reading the arrival column as well.

We would leave on Wednesday at 20:00. That is one night at sea, downwind all the way, with no more than 14 knots of wind and no motoring, and the arrival in Rhodes is at half past eleven in the morning. It is the route in the picture at the top of this post.

Set Avoid wind above to limit the forecast wind speed used for routing. This limit applies to wind speed, not gusts; check the gust forecast separately. In the Wednesday morning example, the strongest wind is 12 knots, but gusts reach 23 knots.

All numbers here come from the forecast of 30 October. If you open the same passage a few days later, it is calculated on the newer forecast and the times will be different.

Changing the route

You may want to change the suggested route. On the way to Rhodes it passes close to Cape Krio on the Turkish coast. If you drag that waypoint further out, the passage is calculated again through the new point; in our example it took about half an hour longer. Right-click or long-press the route to add a waypoint, or a waypoint to remove it. When a leg points into the wind, the planner times it as beating at your boat's best angle. If you change the departure afterwards, the router optimizes the route again around the waypoints you placed.

Saving, sharing and using it underway

  • Save a passage to your bookmarks. When you open it again it is calculated on that day's forecast, so you can see how the plan changes as the departure gets closer.
  • Share sends a link to your crew. They see the same passage, calculated with their own boat.
  • Start the passage when you leave: your GPS position appears next to the planned boat, and IncusGrid tells you when you are 30 minutes ahead or behind the plan, or 2 nautical miles off the route. Adjust the route plans the rest from where you are, and the original plan stays visible for comparison.
  • On a phone the planner is a sheet at the bottom. Fold it down, and the map follows the boat while you move along the route.

Passages longer than the forecast

For an ocean passage the destination is often more than ten days away. In that case the planner does not optimize the route. It draws straight legs through the waypoints you set, calculates them hour by hour as far as the forecast goes, and estimates the rest in grey at your boat's average speed. As the departure gets closer, more of the passage falls within the forecast.

Good to know

The planner uses ECMWF's global forecast and a simple polar. It doesn't yet include currents or the effect of waves on boat speed, and it doesn't route the last miles into the harbour. It is a planning tool, not for navigation.

Without full access, a route covers the first 24 hours of a passage. With full access you can plan the whole passage over all ten days.
