Sailing the Adriatic: A seasonal analysis of lightning patterns

January 28, 2026 by IncusGrid Team

The Adriatic Sea is a dream destination for sailors with stunning coastlines, historic towns, and clear water. However, anyone who has spent a summer in Croatia knows the other side of the coin: Sudden, violent thunderstorms (Neverins) are a real threat.

After a lightning strike hit right next to our boat in Greece, we built IncusGrid to stop guessing and start analyzing. We combined more than a decade of global lightning observations, 30 years of weather reanalysis and detailed terrain models to understand where the real risks lie.

Updated October 2026: All screenshots now come from the current version of IncusGrid. Its Thunderstorm Days layer is based on a new lightning dataset, so we rechecked every statement against it. Some of them changed.

How to read the maps

The Thunderstorm Days layer shows the share of days in a month on which lightning was detected nearby (2013–2025). 10 % means about three thunderstorm days a month, 20 % about six. Each value describes an area of roughly 55 × 40 km. It shows regional patterns, not individual anchorages. For the anchorage itself, there is the terrain layer further down.

The Season Begins: June

By June, thunderstorms are already part of the picture, and not only in the north. The Gulf of Trieste leads with thunderstorms on about 27 % of days. But the south is far from calm: Around Dubrovnik and Budva, it is 19–23 %, about as much as in Split (18 %). The quietest part of the Croatian coast in June is the stretch from Novalja to Zadar, at around 12 %.

The Peak: July & Local Variations

In July, the north stays the most active part of the Adriatic: The Gulf of Trieste and the Kvarner around Rijeka and Senj reach 21–29 % thunderstorm days. Further south, the differences become smaller. Novalja, Šibenik, Split, Makarska and Dubrovnik all sit around 15–17 %.

The calmest stretch is once again around Zadar and Biograd (about 14 %). On the map, it stands out as a lighter band between the Kvarner and Šibenik.

Pro Tip: If you seek calm nights in July, the Zadar archipelago is statistically a slightly safer bet than the Kvarner. The difference to Split is small, though: about one thunderstorm day less per month.

The Contrast: The Ionian Sea

It is also interesting to zoom out. While the Northern Adriatic cooks, the Ionian Sea is remarkably calm in July: around Corfu about 6 % of days see thunderstorms, around Zakynthos only 2 %. For anyone looking to avoid the peak thunderstorm season, it is a real alternative.

High Season: August

In August, the focus shifts south. From Makarska via Dubrovnik to Budva, the coast reaches 21–23 % thunderstorm days, more than in July. The Zadar area remains the calmest part of the Croatian coast at around 16 %. The Gulf of Trieste is still the most active area of the Adriatic (27 %).

Potential is not the same as thunderstorms

IncusGrid also shows a second layer: Thunderstorm Potential, the share of time in which the atmosphere favours thunderstorms (based on 30 years of weather reanalysis). In July, this is the case 55–68 % of the time along the Adriatic coast, but thunderstorms actually occur on only 14–27 % of days.

Both layers therefore answer different questions. The potential shows how often the ingredients are in place, the thunderstorm days show how often a storm actually develops. Where both are high, as in the Gulf of Trieste and the Kvarner, a forecast with a thunderstorm risk deserves extra attention.

Finding Shelter: The Power of Terrain Shielding

Even in active zones, terrain matters. And it matters even within a single bay.

Let's look at the island of Lopud near Dubrovnik with the Terrain Exposure layer. It shows how high the surrounding terrain rises within about 350 m of a spot on the water. Green means strong shielding by nearby hills, purple means little shielding.

The best shielded water around Lopud lies along the south coast below the hills (well over 100 m). In the bay of Uvala Šunj, the picture is mixed: In the inner part, close to the slopes, the terrain rises 40–70 m (light green). In the middle of the bay, it drops to 10–25 m, and at the entrance to almost nothing. The harbour of Lopud town on the other side of the island is in a similar range to Šunj (about 15–50 m).

What this means: If you anchor in Uvala Šunj during a thunderstorm-prone period, the inner part of the bay, close to the slopes, offers more terrain shielding than the middle. The steep hills provide a theoretical "shadow zone", but no guarantee. Always keep an eye on the forecast as well.

Late Season: The Flip (September - November)

As autumn approaches, the Ionian Sea wakes up. In September, Corfu and Zakynthos rise to 13–17 % thunderstorm days, while the Adriatic coast stays at around 20 %.

In October, the pattern flips: The Croatian coast becomes noticeably calmer (about 9–12 % thunderstorm days), while the Ionian Sea gets more active than the Adriatic (16–20 %).

In November, thunderstorm activity increases again in both seas, but the south clearly leads. Around Corfu and Zakynthos, every third day sees thunderstorms (about 30 %). The Croatian coast rises back to 14–19 %, about as much as in July, so the Adriatic is not a calm sea in November either.

Plan your own route

These are general seasonal trends, but every region is different.

Are you planning a charter or a cruise in the Adriatic? Don't rely on luck. Check your specific anchorages on IncusGrid: Use thunderstorm days and potential for the season, and terrain exposure to see whether you are dropping your hook in a sheltered spot or an exposed one.
